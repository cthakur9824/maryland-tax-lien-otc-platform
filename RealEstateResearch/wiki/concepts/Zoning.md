# Zoning

Zoning is a method of urban planning in which a local government divides land into areas called "zones," each of which has a set of regulations for new development. These regulations control:
* **Land Use**: What activities are allowed (residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural).
* **Density**: How many units can be built per acre (e.g., single-family vs. high-rise apartments).
* **Dimensional Standards**: Height limits, building setbacks (distance from property lines), lot coverage percentages, and parking requirements.

---

## Core Types of Zoning

| Zone Type | Standard Code(s) | Description | Development Potential |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Agricultural** | AG, A-1 | Designed to protect farming and open space. Low density (typically 1 home per 20–50 acres). | Low by-right density; high re-zoning upside. |
| **Low-Density Residential** | R-1, R-E | Single-family detached homes. Large lot sizes (0.5 to 2+ acres). | High appeal for custom homebuilders. |
| **Medium/High-Density Residential** | R-2, R-3, R-H | Duplexes, townhomes, garden apartments, and mid-rises. Multiple units per acre. | High demand from production/institutional builders. |
| **Commercial** | C-1, C-2, O-M | Retail, office, restaurants, gas stations, shopping centers. | High value, but limited builder buyer pool. |
| **Mixed-Use** | MXD, MU | Blends residential, commercial, and sometimes cultural uses. | Premium value; highly sought after in urban infill areas. |
| **Industrial** | I-1, M-1 | Manufacturing, warehousing, logistics centers, light assembly. | High value for warehouse builders near highway corridors. |

## Best Zones for Land Flipping to Builders

To make good money flipping land to builders, your goal is to acquire land that has **high density capability** or is **ripe for rezoning / subdivision**. Builders do not want to spend 12–24 months fighting local planning commissions for zoning changes; they pay a premium for **"shovel-ready" (entitled) land**.

Here are the preferred zones for flipping, ranked by builder demand and profit potential:

### 1. Medium to High-Density Residential (R-3, R-H, Townhome Zones)
* **Why builders want it**: Townhome and multifamily developers (like Ryan Homes, Lennar, or regional builders) operate on volume. A lot zoned for R-3 that allows 8–15 townhomes per acre is far more profitable than an R-1 lot that only allows 1 house.
* **Flipping strategy**: Locate contiguous R-1 parcels that can be assembled and rezoned to R-3, or purchase under-utilized properties within city sewer boundaries and rezone them for townhomes before selling to a builder.

### 2. Low-Density Suburban Residential (R-1, R-2)
* **Why builders want it**: Single-family homebuilders are constantly looking for finished lots in suburban school districts.
* **Flipping strategy**: Buy raw land zoned R-1 (e.g., 20 acres), obtain preliminary plat approval to subdivide it into a 15-lot subdivision with utility connections (water/sewer or perc approved), and flip the raw, subdivided plat to a production builder.

### 3. Agricultural (AG) with Subdivision Potential
* **Why builders want it**: Raw agricultural land is cheap. If the county land-use plan indicates the area is slated for future residential growth, it is a prime target for development.
* **Flipping strategy (The Land Entitlement Play)**: 
  1. Buy AG land at a low per-acre price.
  2. Get it rezoned to Residential (R-1/R-2).
  3. Work with engineers to lay out road designs, get perc approvals, or secure sewer allocations.
  4. Sell the entitled land to a developer. This strategy yields the **highest profit margins** in land flipping.

## The Land Flipping Value-Add Matrix

Builders buy land based on **risk mitigation**. The more risk you remove, the higher your profit.

```
[Raw AG Land] -> [Get Perc / Sewer Approved] -> [Rezone to R-1 or R-3] -> [Subdivide / Plat] -> [Shovel-Ready]
  Low Value          (+20% Value)               (+50% Value)             (+100% Value)     (Maximum Premium)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **What to avoid**: Avoid buying land in strictly protected conservation zones, wetlands, or areas designated as permanent agricultural easements (e.g., MALPF in Maryland). These zones have zero flipping potential to production builders because development rights have been legally removed.
