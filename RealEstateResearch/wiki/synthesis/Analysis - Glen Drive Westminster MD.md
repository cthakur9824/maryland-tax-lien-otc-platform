### 2. Critical Development Roadblocks
* **Septic System Infeasibility**: Public records and MLS listing disclosures confirm that the lot **cannot support a standard septic system** under modern Carroll County health codes. Since the area lacks municipal sewer access (relying on private septic systems), a failed or unapprovable percolation ("perc") test makes the lot legally **unbuildable** for residential dwellings. Refer to [[Maryland Mind Map]] for details on Maryland's strict wet-season testing and reserve drainfield rules.
* **Lack of Utilities**: There is no active water well or electric connection set up. Although electric is nearby, setting up a well without a matching septic system yields no development value.
* **Topography & Lot Size**: At 0.56 acres, the lot is relatively small for private septic in Maryland. Carroll County health regulations require a designated primary septic field plus a 10,000-square-foot reserve area, which is highly difficult to fit on a sloped, wooded 0.56-acre parcel that fails soil absorption tests.

## Valuation & Investment Feasibility

To determine if this is worth an offer, we compare it against typical land dynamics in Carroll County:

1. **Comparison with Buildable Lots**:
   * As detailed in [[Maryland Developer Land Deals]], a fully buildable, perc-approved 1.82-acre lot in Carroll County (e.g., New Windsor) sells for approximately $150,000.
   * A buildable 0.5-acre lot in Westminster would typically command $75,000 to $100,000.
2. **Unbuildable Lot Value Discount**:
   * Unbuildable land in residential subdivisions generally loses **85% to 95%** of its market value compared to buildable land.
   * Its utility is restricted to:
     * Adjacent neighbors who want to ensure no one builds next to them and want to absorb the land into their parcel.
     * Storing equipment or passive recreational use (camping, hunting, off-grid storage), though local zoning ordinances in R-20,000 zones often restrict camping or temporary structures.
3. **Target Offer Pricing**:
   * If you are looking to wholesale or flip this to an adjacent neighbor, your purchase price must be low enough to cover holding costs (property taxes, insurance, LLC maintenance) and still leave room for a profit.
   * An offer of **$2,000 to $3,000** is the maximum reasonable price for this level of risk, giving you a chance to resell it to a neighbor for $5,000 to $8,000. Any offer above this (and certainly the seller's cost basis of $20k) is highly unprofitable.

## Conclusion & Next Steps

If you decide to proceed with an offer for speculative/neighbor-wholesale purposes, you should:
1. **Submit a Lowball Offer**: Offer no more than $3,000.
2. **Include Contingencies**: Include a 60-day study period to:
   * Review zoning restrictions regarding accessory structures or non-residential use.
   * Reach out to the immediate neighbors (adjacent property owners) to gauge their interest in buying the parcel to expand their own yards.
3. **Walk Away**: If the seller refuses to drop their price significantly below their $20,345 cost basis, let this deal go. The seller is currently holding an illiquid asset that they cannot build on, and they are looking for a retail buyer to take the loss off their hands.
